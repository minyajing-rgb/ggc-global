More Players, Less Money?

The operating worksheet behind a game’s revenue

1. Count people before multiplying them

A team says it added 10,000 installs today. Someone adds those installs to yesterday’s active audience and calls the total growth. The number looks plausible. It may still describe no identifiable group of people.

An install is an event reported by a store or attribution system. A player is an identity in your product. First open is another event; first meaningful activity is another. Reinstalls, device changes, people who never open and differences between attribution systems can separate these counts. Before judging a campaign, decide which event represents entry into the product you operate.

For a daily operating view, I want three mutually exclusive groups: players active for the first time, continuing or recently active players, and players returning after a defined gap. The gap belongs in the definition. Without it, one analyst may call the same person continuing and another may call that person reactivated.

In our illustrative day, the store reports 10,000 installs. Only 7,000 unique people meet the first-activity rule. There are 26,000 continuing players and 2,000 reactivated players. Daily active users therefore total 35,000. Adding all installs would produce 38,000, overstating this defined population by 3,000.

This does not establish why the other installs failed to qualify. They may reflect delayed first opens, counting differences, friction or poor audience fit. It establishes the next investigation. Compare the event definitions and identity mapping before buying more traffic or redesigning onboarding.

Your first worksheet row should read like a reproducible instruction: product, date, timezone, market, platform, version, identity unit and qualifying activity. Write a reactivation threshold that another analyst can apply. Keep an unresolved definition visible rather than burying it in a spreadsheet footnote.

2. Make the revenue identity hold

For payments made by that same daily active population:

Daily IAP payment amount = DAU × daily payer conversion × daily ARPPU.

Payer conversion is unique daily payers divided by DAU. ARPPU is the daily payment amount divided by those unique payers. The formula works because the period, population and amount basis match. It is an accounting identity for the defined data, not proof that changing one variable will leave the others unchanged.

With 10,000 DAU, daily payer conversion of 3% and daily ARPPU of $8, there are 300 unique payers and $2,400 in daily IAP payments. If each payer makes two purchases, that does not create 600 payers. It creates 600 transactions. Average order value and ARPPU answer different questions.

A common mistake is multiplying daily DAU by monthly payer conversion. Another is using an ARPPU based on a different market or a lifetime payment total. A third is adding advertising to an ARPU that already includes advertising. All can create a fluent presentation with a broken denominator.

Keep web payments and subscriptions explicit. A purchase outside the client may belong to a player who was not active that day. A payment date may differ from an accounting recognition date. Reconcile these items separately; do not force a clean-looking identity by silently changing scope.

When payer conversion changes, inspect perceived value, timing, offer access, price and payment completion. When ARPPU changes, inspect offer mix, frequency and payer composition. The decomposition narrows the question. It does not tell you that a larger discount, a new bundle or a stronger prompt caused the result.

3. Bridge gross amounts to an operating remainder

The most expensive word in an operating meeting can be “revenue” when everyone means a different row.

Use one illustrative day and one product, all amounts in USD:

Bridge row Amount
IAP payments: 10,000 × 3% × $8 $2,400
Advertising amount, on the stated daily basis $600
Combined gross amount $3,000
Assumed IAP deduction: 30% × $2,400 −$720
Amount after that deduction $2,280
Acquisition spending −$1,500
Allocated content and operating spending −$700
Simplified operating remainder $80

The 30% deduction is an assumption for this exercise. It is not a statement about any store’s current fees. The example assumes no additional advertising deduction; a real worksheet must reconcile the advertising amount’s gross or net basis and any further charges.

The $3,000 is not profit. The $80 is not company net profit either. This exercise omits corporate overhead outside the allocation, taxes, currency effects, refunds, recognition differences and settlement timing. Its purpose is to make a specific operating discussion traceable.

Now suppose the acquisition line increases by $100 while every other teaching input remains unchanged. The remainder becomes negative $20. That is a sensitivity calculation, not a forecast that $100 of extra spending produces no extra users or receipts. To make an expansion decision, estimate and then measure the incremental users and value associated with that spending.

A different sensitivity is a $100 increase in gross IAP payments. Under this exercise’s 30% IAP deduction and unchanged other rows, the remainder rises by $70, to $150. A $100 increase in the advertising row would have a different effect under the stated assumptions. “Revenue increased by $100” is therefore insufficient to explain the operating change.

Finally, give cash its own timeline. The ad bill may be payable before the store settles proceeds. A profitable cohort on a defined contribution basis can still create a funding gap. Record the actual dates rather than treating a recognized amount as cash in the bank.

4. Give acquisition cohorts the same clock

Lifetime value needs a horizon. Realized value through day seven and predicted lifetime value should not share an unlabeled column.

For a defined cohort:

Realized value per original user through day N = cumulative cohort amount through day N ÷ original unique cohort users.

Specify whether the observation window is D0–D6, seven elapsed days or a rolling 168 hours. Define the timezone and how incomplete days are handled. Keep a mature cohort’s seven-day result separate from a recent cohort with only three days observed.

Acquisition cost also needs a denominator. Cost per install, cost per first active player and cost per first payer are different measures. Comparing cost per install with LTV per payer can make an unviable campaign look attractive.

Imagine an illustrative cohort of 1,000 first active players acquired for $1,500. If cumulative value on the defined recoverable basis is $900 by the end of the agreed seven-day window, realized value is $0.90 per original player and acquisition cost is $1.50 per first active player. That cohort has not recovered acquisition spending within the observed window. Nothing in those inputs identifies the future recovery date.

This additional cohort example is a new teaching scenario for the companion; it is not a recovered historical result from Joyce’s narration. A forecast can be added in a separate column with its retention, monetization and cost assumptions. Do not replace the measured shortfall with a confident date simply because a model can extend a curve.

Segment by entry date, market, channel, creative promise and product version. Before attributing a change to the campaign, ask whether those conditions are comparable. A blended average can conceal an attractive segment and a loss-making segment.

5. Turn an unexplained change into a decision

“The dashboard looks better” is a description with no owner. A useful operating statement connects an observed change to a bounded next action.

Start with one question. For example: did a change in first-session offer timing improve completed purchases among a defined new-player cohort? Keep the event window and eligible population fixed. Record what else changed, including acquisition mix, price, version, payment availability and event calendar.

Then separate three layers:

  • Observation: what the defined events and amounts actually show.
  • Hypothesis: a possible explanation, including competing explanations.
  • Decision: the next action, owner, cost limit, observation window and evidence that would change the plan.

Do not infer willingness to pay from a button click alone. Check the path through checkout completion, payment confirmation, refunds and the promised entitlement. More clicks can expose an appealing offer followed by a broken payment flow.

Likewise, do not infer durable value from a short payment spike. Check return behavior and later purchasing on the same cohort basis. A change that increases payments today may shift timing, change payer composition or impose costs that appear later.

This worksheet does not prescribe a universal experiment sample size or a statistical stopping threshold. Those depend on the outcome, baseline, detectable effect, variance and experimental design. The first practical step is to make the question measurable; the next is to choose a design that can answer it.

6. Complete a weekly operating review

Use the supplied CSV to bring one week of your own product data into a common language. Keep private player identifiers and customer records outside any shared review file.

  1. Write the date window, timezone, product, market, platform and version.
  2. Define first activity, continuing activity and the return gap. Reconcile the mutually exclusive groups to DAU.
  3. Reconcile unique payers, transactions and IAP payments. Calculate daily conversion and daily ARPPU only on the same scope.
  4. Add advertising and other actual models once, with gross/net labels.
  5. Build the deduction and spending bridge; state what the remainder excludes.
  6. Add settlement and payment dates to a separate cash view.
  7. Compare mature cohorts with the same horizon and denominator.
  8. Choose one input to investigate and assign an owner, observation window and stop or adjustment rule.

A useful review ends with a decision that another person can reproduce from the definitions and evidence. The model connects product, publishing, data and team delivery because each person can see which input their work is meant to change.
