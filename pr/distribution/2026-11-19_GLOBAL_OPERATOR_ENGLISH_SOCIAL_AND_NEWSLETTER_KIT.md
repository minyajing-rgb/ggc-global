## LinkedIn — flagship operator post (best first)

**Hook:** A successful launch is not an operating system.

A game can have excellent art, a stable build, and an impressive launch deck — and still fail the business test in a second market.

I’ve spent much of my career working on the less photogenic part of games: the operating decisions behind the product.

At Zynga I held product and commercial responsibility across social and casual games. At Gameloft my Asian operating role involved regional and global franchise work spanning more than ten studios and a broad cross-functional network. Later I built game development and publishing businesses.

Different companies, different products, same recurring question:

**Who can explain why the business should invest its next dollar — and who can change the plan when the evidence disagrees?**

My framework starts with three distinct receipts:

1. **Build evidence.** The game works in the declared environment.
2. **First-play evidence.** Someone who did not build it can enter, understand, play and complete the first meaningful action.
3. **Audience evidence.** Defined, deduplicated users return or convert in comparable cohorts.

These are not interchangeable.

And as AI lowers the cost of making content and prototypes, the cost of making the wrong operating decision matters even more.

I wrote a practitioner note on this gap — and why teams need a named owner for go / improve / stop, not just another polished dashboard.

## LinkedIn — publisher/developer executive post

**Hook:** The most expensive part of a publishing agreement can be the operating handoff.

Most publishing negotiations obsess over terms. That matters. But there is another test worth asking before anyone signs:

**Who makes the first 90 days work?**

When a studio owns the game and a publisher owns the regional market, four decisions must have explicit owners:

- What counts as a valid, unassisted first-play experience in the target region?
- Whose cohort definitions and analytics can justify increasing the marketing budget?
- Who has the authority to modify the economy, content calendar, localization and creative promises?
- When early market evidence changes, who can say *pause* rather than simply ask for more spend?

A contract is not an operating rhythm. An impressive build is not a retention cohort. A market estimate is not a profit-and-loss statement.

This is one reason I increasingly frame global publishing around decision rights, not just distribution.

I’m collecting practical examples and would welcome a thoughtful exchange with operators, founders and publishing leads on where handoffs have worked — or failed — across borders.
